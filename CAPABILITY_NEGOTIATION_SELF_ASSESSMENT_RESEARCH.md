# Capability Negotiation — Can an LLM Usefully Assess Its Own Suitability?

**Ugence Labs | The Governed AI Platform**
*A falsification-first investigation into whether LLM self-assessment is a useful input to enterprise model routing — or merely another heuristic dressed as an architecture.*
*Version 1.0 — July 2026*

> **What this document is.** A research investigation of one hypothesis: *can an LLM assess its own
> capability for a task accurately enough that its self-assessment becomes a useful input into
> enterprise routing?* It is **not** a protocol proposal, **not** production code, and **not** an
> expansion of the Model Selection Policy specification. It is a critical inquiry whose default posture
> is skepticism: the burden of proof is on self-assessment to demonstrate marginal value, not on the
> critic to disprove it.
>
> **Relationship to the Policy Engine.** The Model Selection Policy specification
> (`MODEL_SELECTION_POLICY_ENGINE_SPEC.md`) established a four-class provenance model —
> **declared / measured / observed**, plus enterprise governance. This document asks whether
> **model self-assessment** deserves to be a *fifth* source feeding that policy, and if so, with what
> trust weight and in what regime. It deliberately does not re-open the policy design.
>
> **Reading discipline.** Claims are labeled: **[EVIDENCE]** where grounded in the published behavior
> of LLM calibration/self-knowledge; **[ARGUMENT]** for architectural reasoning; **[METHOD]** for a
> proposed experiment; **[SPEC]** for a design sketch. The conclusion (Part 7) states, in both
> directions, exactly what evidence would overturn it.

## 0. Thesis and falsification-first summary

**The hypothesis, stated so it can be attacked:** *A model, asked in advance whether it can handle a
task, returns an assessment accurate enough to improve routing beyond what a static registry, observed
telemetry, and enterprise policy already provide.*

**Four attacks, applied before any support (detailed in Part 1):**

1. **No privileged access.** A self-assessment is produced by the same weights that would do the task.
   It is a *prediction generated by the model*, not introspection into a separate faculty. A model that
   hallucinates on the task can hallucinate equally on "can I do this task?" Self-assessment is not a
   more reliable channel; it is the same channel pointed at a different prompt. **[ARGUMENT]**
2. **Calibration is poor where it matters.** Post-RLHF models are systematically overconfident and
   their *verbalized* confidence ("88%") is weakly calibrated, clusters at round numbers, and is pushed
   upward by helpfulness/sycophancy bias. The signal is not zero, but it is noisy and biased toward
   "yes, I can." **[EVIDENCE]**
3. **The model cannot know most routing-relevant facts.** Cost, latency, live context limits,
   deployment location, compliance posture, its own exact version — these are infrastructure facts. A
   model asked its own price or context window frequently confabulates. Self-assessment is only even
   *plausible* for a narrow subset of capabilities, and that subset is precisely the one where it is
   least reliable: its own competence. **[ARGUMENT]**
4. **Structural incentive to say yes.** A model being asked "are you suitable?" as a gate to being
   *used* has, at minimum, an RLHF helpfulness bias toward acceptance, and — if the provider is aware
   of the routing use — a commercial incentive toward it. Self-selection bias is baked in. **[ARGUMENT]**

**Where the attacks do not fully land — the residual signal:**

- Self-assessment about **task shape** ("this needs decomposition / external tools / retrieval") is
  more reliable than self-assessment about **self-competence**, because it is a claim about the task,
  not a claim about the model's own quality.
- After **post-hoc recalibration against telemetry**, a model's confidence can retain *residual*
  predictive value beyond a static registry — small, but non-zero.
- The one regime where self-assessment may be the *only* available signal is **cold start**: a brand-new
  task type or a brand-new model, before any telemetry exists.

**The verdict this document defends (Part 7):** Capability Negotiation is **useful advisory metadata,
regime-dependent and decaying — not a first-class architectural layer.** Its value is real but bounded
to (a) the *self-knowledge* provenance class only, (b) captured offline and *verified against
telemetry*, never trusted at face value, and (c) weighted meaningfully only at cold start, decaying
toward zero as observed measurements accumulate. Elevating it to a runtime layer between the Policy
Engine and the Router is an anti-pattern: it reintroduces a latency/cost tax and inverts the trust
model. **Capability Negotiation is a data-acquisition method, not an architectural layer.**

## 1. Part 1 — Can models accurately assess themselves?

The task lists eleven things a model might self-assess. The decisive first move is to split them by
*whether the model could even in principle know the answer* — because most of them the model cannot.

| Candidate self-assessment | Can the model genuinely know it? | Reliability of self-report |
|---|---|---|
| **Reasoning suitability** | Partially — it is predicting its own future performance | Low–Medium; overconfidence-biased |
| **Coding suitability** | Partially — same | Low–Medium; overconfidence-biased |
| **Context limitations** (will this fit / degrade?) | The *limit* is an infra fact; the *degradation* is partly knowable | Limit: model unreliable, infra authoritative. Degradation: weak self-signal |
| **Structured-output capability** | The model knows whether it *tends* to produce valid schema, weakly | Low; contradicted by measured validity rates |
| **Tool-usage capability** | Weakly — it can say it "would use a tool," not that it calls reliably | Low; measured tool-call reliability dominates |
| **Hallucination risk** | This is the paradox: asking a model to predict its own hallucination | Very low; the failure mode assessing itself |
| **Confidence** | It can emit a number | Low–Medium calibration; recalibratable |
| **Expected latency** | **No** — infra fact (hardware, load, provider) | ~Zero; must not come from the model |
| **Expected cost** | **No** — infra/pricing fact | ~Zero; confabulation-prone |
| **Deployment restrictions** | **No** — enterprise/infra fact | ~Zero; must not come from the model |
| **Compliance restrictions** | **No** — enterprise/legal fact | ~Zero; dangerous if self-asserted |

**The pattern is stark.** Of eleven, **five are pure infrastructure/enterprise facts the model cannot
know** (latency, cost, deployment, compliance, and the hard context *limit*), and asking the model for
them is not merely useless but actively harmful — a confident wrong answer is worse than no answer. Of
the remaining six, all are *predictions of the model's own future behavior*, and the one most valuable
for safety — hallucination risk — is the one the model is least equipped to make, because it is asking
the failure mode to audit itself.

**What the model can genuinely contribute — narrowly.** The reliable residue is not "am I good enough?"
but **"what is the shape of this task?"**:

- *Decomposition / strategy* — "this contract-analysis task should be split into clause extraction then
  cross-clause reasoning." This is a claim about the task, verifiable independently, and models are
  reasonably good at it. **[EVIDENCE]**
- *Explicit tool/retrieval need* — "this requires a calculator / current data / a document I was not
  given." Again a task-shape claim, not a self-competence claim.
- *Named anticipated weaknesses* — "I may struggle with the jurisdiction-specific statutory
  references," which becomes a *verifiable warning*, not a self-grade.

**Separation of concerns — what the model knows vs. what only infrastructure knows:**

```
   MODEL CAN PREDICT (weakly, must verify)     INFRASTRUCTURE/ENTERPRISE KNOWS (authoritatively)
   ────────────────────────────────────────    ──────────────────────────────────────────────────
   task decomposition & strategy                context window limit
   need for external tools / retrieval          price per token
   anticipated task-specific weaknesses          measured latency (TTFT, tps)
   raw (uncalibrated) confidence                 measured hallucination / schema-validity rates
   qualitative difficulty of the task            deployment location & residency
                                                  compliance certifications
                                                  approved-provider list
                                                  historical success / retry telemetry
```

**Conclusion of Part 1.** A model can contribute a *narrow band* of task-shape predictions and a
*poorly-calibrated* confidence signal. It cannot know the majority of routing-relevant facts, and for
those it must be structurally forbidden from asserting them. Any protocol that lets a model answer
"expected_cost" or "compliance" is designing in a confabulation surface. **[ARGUMENT]**

## 2. Part 2 — Information provenance: who can actually know each thing?

This is the analytical core. Every capability must be tagged with **who has authoritative knowledge of
it** — and self-assessment is authoritative for almost nothing.

| Provenance class | Authoritative for | Examples | Trust in a self-assessment for this class |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Provider Metadata** | The provider / infra polling | context window, pricing, tool support, deployment options, rate limits, API version | **Reject self-report.** Model confabulates; poll the provider. |
| **Model Self-Knowledge** | The model (as a *prediction*) | task difficulty, recommended decomposition, expected weaknesses, need for external tools | **The only class where the model is a legitimate source — and even here it is an unverified claim.** |
| **Observed Measurements** | Our production telemetry | latency, hallucination rate, schema validity, retry frequency, success rate | **Reject self-report where telemetry exists.** Telemetry strictly dominates. |
| **Enterprise Policy** | The enterprise | approved providers, privacy tier, regulation, customer rules | **Reject self-report categorically.** The model must never assert policy. |

**The single most important finding of this document:** self-assessment is a legitimate source for
**exactly one of four provenance classes**, and even there it produces a *prediction to be verified*,
not a fact. For the other three classes, self-assessment is not merely redundant — it is a **trust
hazard**, because a fluent, confident wrong answer is harder to catch than a missing one.

**Additional provenance sources beyond the four listed (the task asked whether others exist):**

- **Request-time computed context.** The *actual* input token count, the *actual* required output-schema
  complexity, presence of images — these are computed from the payload, not assessed by anyone. They
  are more authoritative than any self-report about "context limitations." **[ARGUMENT]**
- **Cross-model / consensus signals.** A cheap judge model's second opinion, or agreement across
  candidate models, is a distinct source from any single model's self-assessment — and less prone to
  one model's idiosyncratic bias.
- **Third-party / community benchmarks.** External evals — authoritative-ish at publication, **decaying
  in validity after every model update** (a first-class caveat: a benchmark measured on version N is
  not valid for silently-updated version N+1).
- **Provider changelogs / deprecation notices.** The authoritative source for capability *changes* over
  time — again, not the model.

**Implication for architecture.** Because self-assessment covers only the *Model Self-Knowledge* class,
its natural home is to **populate that one class in the registry** — provenance-tagged, verified against
Observed Measurements, and never permitted to leak into the other three classes. This already tells us
(previewing Part 6) that Capability Negotiation is a *source feeding the policy's existing provenance
model*, not a new layer.

## 3. Part 3 — Capability Negotiation Protocol

The task is explicit that inventing the protocol is *not* the objective — so this section is a
disciplined sketch whose main purpose is to encode the Part 1–2 findings as hard constraints, not to
optimize field names.

### 3.1 Design principles (derived from the findings)

1. **Scope enforcement.** The protocol must **forbid the model from asserting Provider-Metadata,
   Observed-Measurement, or Enterprise-Policy fields.** The model is handed those as *inputs* (so it can
   reason about fit) and may only *return* Model-Self-Knowledge fields. A returned `expected_cost` or
   `compliance` value is a protocol violation, auto-rejected. This is the protocol's whole point.
2. **Three-valued knowledge, explicitly.** Every field the model returns must permit
   `known | unknown | not_applicable`. `"unknown"` must be a *first-class, rewarded* answer so the
   model can decline to confabulate. Absent this, RLHF pressure fills every field with a plausible
   guess. **Unknown is the safety valve.**
3. **Claims carry a verifiable basis.** Confidence is useless without a rationale that can later be
   checked against outcomes; every substantive claim ships with a short `basis` string enabling Part 4
   detection.
4. **Determinism.** Temperature 0, schema-constrained decoding, fixed prompt — so the same
   `(model_version, task_profile, protocol_version)` yields the same assessment and is replayable.
5. **Small footprint, optional fields.** Fields are optional; a model returning fewer, higher-quality
   claims is preferred to one padding every field. Token cost of the preflight is a real tax (Part 6).
6. **Versionable and bound.** The record binds `protocol_version` × `model_version` — because an
   assessment is only meaningful relative to the exact model that produced it, and models change
   silently.

### 3.2 Should it be JSON? Should fields be optional? How are unknowns represented?

- **JSON: yes** — with schema-enforced/constrained decoding, for determinism and machine consumption,
  consistent with the Policy Engine's decision-record format. The negotiation record is data, not prose.
- **Optional: yes** — and more strongly, *`unknown` must be distinguishable from absent*. Absent = the
  model didn't address the field; `unknown` = the model explicitly disclaims knowledge. These carry
  different information for Part 4 (a model that says `unknown` is behaving correctly; one that omits may
  be evading).
- **Unknown representation:** an explicit sentinel, e.g. `{"value": null, "known": false}`, never an
  empty string or a fabricated default. The distinction *known-false vs. unknown vs. not-applicable* is
  the difference between a usable protocol and a confabulation engine.

### 3.3 Sketch [SPEC]

```json
// INPUT: infra provides the facts; the model is NOT asked to supply them.
{
  "protocol_version": "1.0",
  "task_profile": {                      // provider-metadata + policy: INPUTS, not questions
    "task": "contract_analysis", "domain": "legal",
    "input_tokens": 180000, "requires_structured_output": true
  },
  "model_context": { "model_version": "vendorX-modelY-2026-05" }
}

// OUTPUT: the model may return ONLY self-knowledge fields, each with known/unknown.
{
  "protocol_version": "1.0",
  "model_version": "vendorX-modelY-2026-05",
  "self_assessment": {
    "suitability": { "value": "suitable_with_strategy", "known": true, "confidence": 72,
                     "basis": "task type familiar; jurisdiction-specific statutes are a known weak area" },
    "recommended_strategy": { "value": "clause-level extraction then cross-clause reasoning",
                              "known": true },
    "external_tool_need": { "value": ["statute_lookup"], "known": true },
    "anticipated_weaknesses": { "value": ["non-US jurisdictions"], "known": true },
    "self_estimated_context_fit": { "value": null, "known": false }   // correctly declines an infra fact
  },
  "refused_fields": ["expected_cost", "expected_latency", "compliance", "deployment"]  // by protocol
}
```

The protocol is deliberately thin. Its value is not the field list; it is the **enforced scoping** (the
model cannot speak outside its provenance class) and the **first-class `unknown`**. Everything hard
about this workstream is downstream of the protocol, in whether the returned self-knowledge is *true*.

## 4. Part 4 — Failure analysis and detection

The unifying observation: **every failure mode of self-assessment is detected the same way — by
comparing the claim against an authoritative source or an observed outcome.** That single fact bounds
the architecture (Part 6): self-assessment can never be safe *alone*, because its safety is
manufactured entirely by the other sources.

| Failure | What happens | Detection |
|---|---|---|
| **Model overestimates itself** (false "suitable") | Task routed to it, fails or needs rework | Track **assessment-vs-outcome divergence**: per model × task family, compare declared suitability/confidence to observed success. Calibration/Brier tracking; alert when confidence decouples from success. |
| **Model underestimates itself** (false "unsuitable") | A capable model is skipped; cost/quality left on table | Hardest to detect — the counterfactual isn't observed. Use **periodic shadow challenges**: occasionally route a task to a model that self-declined and measure. Bounded, budgeted. |
| **Provider silently changes the model** | Prior assessments and benchmarks now invalid | **Version fingerprinting + a canary regression suite.** Detect drift in assessment *or* outcome for a fixed prompt set; invalidate the self-knowledge cache on change. |
| **Pricing changes** | — | **Not a self-assessment field at all** (provenance = provider metadata). Detected by infra polling; the protocol forbids the model from asserting it, so it cannot fail here. |
| **Context limit changes** | — | Same — provider metadata; polled, not negotiated. The protocol's scoping (Part 3.1) prevents this class of failure by construction. |
| **API deprecation** | Model disappears | Health/lifecycle monitoring in the registry, independent of negotiation. |
| **Hallucinated capability claim** | Model asserts a capability it lacks (e.g. a modality, a tool) | **Verify every claim against the authoritative registry.** Any self-asserted *provider-metadata* field is auto-rejected (Part 3.1); any self-asserted *capability* is trusted only after telemetry confirms it. |
| **Strategic / helpfulness bias** ("yes I can") | Systematic overacceptance | Structural, not per-call: **self-assessment is never the sole or dominant input**, and is continuously recalibrated against outcomes so a persistent optimistic bias is measured and subtracted. Cross-provider comparison surfaces one model's idiosyncratic inflation. |

**The detection asymmetry that matters.** Overestimation is cheap to detect (you ran the task and saw
it fail). Underestimation is expensive (you never ran it). This asymmetry means a naive system will
*silently accumulate underestimation* — capable-but-shy models quietly starved of traffic — unless
deliberate shadow-challenge exploration is budgeted. Any deployment of Capability Negotiation must fund
that exploration or it will drift. **[ARGUMENT]**

## 5. Part 5 — Evaluation design

### 5.1 The four arms — and the arm the task didn't name but that decides everything

The requested comparison is A (registry only) / B (benchmark only) / C (self-assessment only) /
D (hybrid). But A-vs-B-vs-C-vs-D cannot answer the actual question, which is **marginal value**. The
decisive arm is a fifth:

- **A** — Static registry only
- **B** — Historical benchmark/telemetry only
- **C** — Self-assessment only
- **D** — Full hybrid (registry + self-assessment + observed + policy)
- **D⁻** — **Hybrid *minus* self-assessment** (registry + observed + policy) ← *the ablation that matters*

**The real experiment is D vs. D⁻.** If the full hybrid does not beat the hybrid-without-self-assessment,
then self-assessment carries **no marginal information** and the entire hypothesis fails, regardless of
how C compares to A or B. Comparing C to A/B measures self-assessment *in isolation*; only D-vs-D⁻
measures whether it adds anything the other sources don't already have. **[METHOD]**

### 5.2 Stratify by regime — the predicted crux

Split every metric by telemetry availability:

- **Cold-start stratum** — new task type or new model, little/no observed telemetry.
- **Warm stratum** — rich telemetry exists.

**Pre-registered prediction [METHOD]:** self-assessment's marginal value (D − D⁻) is **positive and
meaningful in the cold-start stratum** and **≈ zero in the warm stratum**, because once Observed
Measurements exist they strictly dominate a self-reported prediction. If this stratification is not
done, a warm-heavy test set will wash out whatever cold-start value exists, and a cold-heavy set will
overstate it. This split is the difference between a fair test and a misleading one.

### 5.3 Metrics (all reported per stratum)

| Metric | What it tells us about self-assessment |
|---|---|
| **Selection regret** vs. an oracle over eligible models | The north star: does adding self-assessment reduce regret? |
| **Routing accuracy** (chose the best eligible model) | Direct routing quality |
| **Task success rate** | Downstream validation of the choice |
| **Operational cost per *successful* task** | Includes the **preflight token tax** — self-assessment must pay for itself |
| **Latency** | Includes the **extra preflight round-trip** — a real cost of any runtime negotiation |
| **Fallback frequency** | Does self-assessment reduce or increase re-routing? |
| **Human-review rate** | Does it catch unsuitability earlier, or add noise? |

Two metrics — cost and latency — must be charged the **preflight tax** explicitly. A preflight call that
marginally improves selection but doubles round-trips and adds token cost may be net-negative even when
its *accuracy* contribution is positive. Evaluations that ignore the tax will overstate value.

### 5.4 Method

Counterfactual / off-policy evaluation (shadow routing, logged-bandit / inverse-propensity estimation),
held-out task distribution not used for tuning, per-source calibration curves, and an explicit
**incremental-information test**: does self-assessment confidence predict residual success variance
*after* conditioning on registry + telemetry? If the partial correlation is ≈ 0, there is no marginal
signal. **[METHOD]**

## 6. Part 6 — Is Capability Negotiation a first-class architectural layer?

### 6.1 Is it different from existing routers?

**In mechanism, yes.** LiteLLM and Portkey route on operator-authored config and provider metadata;
OpenRouter routes on price/availability across a marketplace; Bedrock, Vertex AI, and Azure AI Foundry
provide model catalogs, guardrails, and (some) latency/load-based routing. **None of them performs a
preflight self-assessment call in which the model is asked to evaluate its own suitability.** So
Capability Negotiation is not a feature any of them ships; it is genuinely a different mechanism.

**But different ≠ valuable, and different ≠ a layer.** The question the task poses is whether it is a
*new architectural layer* or *merely another heuristic*. The analysis in Parts 1–4 forces a specific
answer:

### 6.2 Why it should NOT be a layer between Policy Engine and Router

Placing Capability Negotiation as a runtime layer *between* the Policy Engine and the Model Router means
the router, before selecting, calls candidate models to ask "are you suitable?" This design has three
structural defects:

1. **Latency and cost tax at the worst possible time.** A preflight call per candidate, on the hot
   path, for information a registry answers in microseconds. To assess *k* candidates you pay *k* extra
   round-trips before doing any work. **[ARGUMENT]**
2. **Trust inversion.** It puts a *low-trust, verify-later* signal on the *critical, decide-now* path.
   Everything in Parts 1–4 says self-assessment must be verified against authoritative sources before
   it is trusted — which cannot happen at route-time. A layer that must be verified after the fact does
   not belong on the synchronous decision path.
3. **Redundancy in the warm regime.** Part 5's prediction is that self-assessment adds ≈ 0 once
   telemetry exists — i.e., for the overwhelming majority of production traffic, the layer would do work
   that changes nothing.

### 6.3 What it actually is: a data-acquisition method feeding the existing provenance model

The correct placement follows directly from Part 2: self-assessment populates the **Model
Self-Knowledge** provenance class of the registry the Policy Engine already consumes. Concretely:

- **Offline / asynchronous, not route-time.** Negotiation is run when a model is onboarded, when a new
  task family appears, or on a refresh cadence — *not* per request. The result is cached, provenance-
  tagged self-knowledge in the registry.
- **Verified against telemetry, decaying in weight.** Its trust weight is high only where telemetry is
  absent (cold start) and decays toward zero as Observed Measurements accumulate — exactly the
  declared→measured→observed precedence the Policy spec already defines.
- **Scoped to one class.** It never supplies cost, latency, deployment, or compliance; those remain
  provider-metadata / policy inputs.

Under this placement, Capability Negotiation is **not a new layer** — it is a **fifth input source**
into the Model Selection Policy's existing fusion, sitting *behind* the Policy Engine as data, not
*between* it and the router as control flow.

### 6.4 Verdict for Part 6

Capability Negotiation is a **genuinely novel data-acquisition mechanism** (no existing router does it)
but **not a genuinely novel architectural layer** (it must not sit on the route-time path). Framed as a
layer, it is an anti-pattern; framed as a decaying, verified, cold-start-weighted source into the
existing policy, it is a modest, defensible enhancement. The distinction between *mechanism* and *layer*
is the whole answer to the task's central question. **[ARGUMENT]**

## 7. Part 7 — Recommendation, and what would falsify it

### 7.1 Recommendation

Against the task's four possible outcomes:

> **Outcome 2, trending to a bounded Outcome 3 — "useful advisory metadata, and useful enough to
> influence routing *only in the cold-start regime, only for the self-knowledge class, and only after
> verification*." Explicitly not Outcome 4 (not a first-class layer), and not Outcome 1 (not valueless).**

The reasoning, compressed:

- **Not Outcome 1 (no value):** self-assessment about *task shape* (decomposition, tool need,
  anticipated weaknesses) is genuinely informative, and at cold start it may be the only signal
  available. There is a non-zero, orthogonal contribution — in one narrow band.
- **Not Outcome 4 (strong first-class layer):** Parts 1–4 show it covers only one of four provenance
  classes, is systematically biased, must be verified against the other sources to be safe, and — placed
  on the route-time path — imposes a latency/cost tax for information that is redundant the moment
  telemetry exists. It is a source, not a layer.
- **The honest middle (Outcome 2→3):** treat model self-assessment as a **low-trust, provenance-scoped,
  telemetry-verified, cold-start-weighted input** to the Model Selection Policy — the fifth source that
  fills the gap the other four leave *only* before observation exists, and yields to telemetry as soon
  as telemetry arrives.

**On the meta-question ("does combining all four sources beat any single source?"):** almost certainly
yes — but that is a property of *multi-source fusion*, which the Policy spec already asserts, not a
special property of self-assessment. Self-assessment's specific marginal contribution to that fusion is
**small, biased, and regime-dependent**, and must be *measured (D vs. D⁻, stratified), never assumed.*
The robust claim is "fusion beats any single source"; the fragile claim is "self-assessment is a
load-bearing part of that fusion." This document supports the first and is skeptical of the second.

### 7.2 What would falsify this conclusion — stated in both directions

**Evidence that would push the conclusion *down* toward Outcome 1 (no value):**
- In the evaluation, **D ≈ D⁻ even in the cold-start stratum** — self-assessment adds no marginal
  information the registry and telemetry don't already carry, ever.
- The **incremental-information test** (Part 5.4) shows self-assessment confidence has ≈ 0 partial
  correlation with residual success after conditioning on registry + telemetry.
- Overacceptance bias proves **uncorrectable** — recalibration against outcomes fails to stabilize
  because the bias shifts with every model update faster than telemetry can re-fit it.

**Evidence that would push the conclusion *up* toward Outcome 4 (first-class layer):**
- Post-calibration self-assessment predicts task success **better than the full registry + telemetry
  stack even in the warm regime**, *and* does so cheaply enough that the accuracy gain survives the
  preflight latency/cost tax. (This would mean the model knows something durable that observation does
  not capture — a strong, surprising result.)
- Underestimation-driven starvation turns out to be **negligible without shadow challenges**, removing
  the main operational cost of trusting self-assessment.

Until such evidence exists, the disciplined position is: **build Capability Negotiation as an offline,
verified, decaying source into the Model Selection Policy — and prove its marginal value with the D-vs-D⁻
ablation before granting it any weight on the routing decision.** The mechanism is novel; the layer is
not; the value is real but bounded, and the burden of proof stays on the signal.
